“Did the program work?” is a reasonable question. It is also several questions wearing one coat. What changed? For whom? Compared with what? And did the change matter to the people involved?

Separate activity from impact.

Suppose a community health program runs a series of workshops. Counting sessions and attendance tells us something about delivery. It does not, by itself, tell us whether participants gained useful knowledge, found support, or experienced better health.

It helps to sketch the path from activity to intended change. What should happen immediately? What might take longer? What assumptions connect one step to the next? That sketch gives the evaluation something more useful to test than “a lot of things happened.”

Look beyond the average.

An overall improvement can sit alongside very different experiences. A program might work well for people with flexible schedules while remaining difficult to access for people with caring responsibilities or unreliable transport.

Useful questions include:

  • Who took part, and who could not?
  • Do outcomes differ across groups or circumstances?
  • What do participants consider a meaningful improvement?
  • What burden did taking part create?

Looking at smaller groups also needs care. Small samples create uncertainty, and detailed reporting can put privacy at risk. More detail is useful only when it is appropriate to share.

Ask what else could explain it.

A change after a program is not automatically a change caused by the program. Other services, seasonal patterns, or changes in who participates might contribute. The strength of a conclusion should match the strength of the evidence.

Numbers and participant accounts can help illuminate different parts of the story. Together, they can point toward a practical next step: keep something, change something, or ask a better question.

The aim is a clearer picture of people’s experiences. A single tidy percentage rarely gets the whole job done.
