During episode #2526 of The Joe Rogan Experience, Joe Rogan and Vice President JD Vance discussed voter ID laws as though the question were fairly straightforward: if you want fair elections, why wouldn't you require everyone to show identification?

At first glance, I understand the argument. I'm generally in favor of standardization. Consistent procedures can improve accountability, allow meaningful comparisons, and help us identify better practices.

But there's a distinction worth making: a policy sounding reasonable doesn't mean it solves the problem it claims to address.

And this is where the conversation gets interesting.

What does the evidence actually say?

According to a 2025 Pew Research Center survey, 95% of Republicans and 71% of Democrats support requiring government-issued photo ID to vote.

So voter ID is hardly an idea that only conservatives support.

But popularity isn't evidence of effectiveness.

A 2012 News21 investigation examined 2,068 alleged election-fraud cases and identified just 10 cases of in-person voter impersonation, the specific type of fraud photo ID requirements at polling places are designed to address.

Even the Heritage Foundation's 2017 database report, frequently cited by voter-fraud advocates, documented 1,071 cases across multiple years, states, and types of election misconduct. These included registration fraud and other offenses that showing an ID at a polling station wouldn't necessarily prevent.

More importantly, a 2021 study by Cantoni and Pons, using a nationwide voter panel, found no significant effect of strict voter ID laws on actual or perceived election fraud.

Interestingly, the same study also found no significant reduction in turnout overall or across racial groups. That's worth acknowledging. Evidence matters even when it complicates our preferred narrative.

Still, if a policy doesn't demonstrably reduce fraud, shouldn't we be asking what we're accomplishing by implementing it?

The question of access

It's easy to wonder why someone wouldn't have identification when obtaining it has never been particularly difficult for you.

But that's not everyone's reality.

A 2024 University of Maryland-led study estimated that nearly 2.6 million voting-age American citizens lack any government-issued photo ID.

Others face difficulties involving costs, missing documents, transportation, disabilities, or outdated information.

And those barriers aren't distributed equally across income and racial groups.

This is where my concerns about classism and structural racism enter the conversation. Not because asking for identification is inherently racist, but because a supposedly neutral requirement can impose very different burdens on different people.

The same concern applies to xenophobic narratives that turn immigrants into convenient suspects without establishing evidence of widespread illegal voting.

Better standards, not just more barriers

Rogan and Vance also suggested that voter ID could address mail-in ballot fraud. But identification at one point in the process doesn't automatically prevent ballot tampering, improper collection, or other misconduct occurring later.

If we're serious about election integrity, why not prioritize standardized reporting, transparent audits, accurate voter rolls, accessible identification, and clear procedures for verifying ballots?

I'd support reforms that demonstrably improve security while keeping voting accessible, including reasonable identification standards when adequate alternatives and safeguards exist.

What I struggle with is treating another bureaucratic hurdle as an accomplishment in itself.

Election integrity and voting access shouldn't be competing objectives. A functioning democracy requires both.

And if we're going to redesign a system, I'd rather start with the evidence than with what sounds logical on a podcast.
